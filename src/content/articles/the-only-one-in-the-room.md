Have you ever been in a room where your background, identity, or experience set you apart from everyone else? Did that make you more conscious of how you might be perceived?

For me, that awareness has sometimes come from being the only Black person, or one of very few people of color, in a professional setting. That has been my experience throughout my career, but some personal experiences over the past couple of years have made that pressure feel heavier.

Listening and taking time to process are part of my natural approach. There have also been times when I’ve held back, concerned that a question, an unfinished thought, or a different perspective might be interpreted through assumptions about my ability.

I cannot know what others are thinking, but weighing how a contribution might be received takes attention away from the conversation itself.

An impression can begin forming before a person enters the room. Rhetoric about who is qualified or belongs can shape how an individual is seen, especially when claims go unchecked. By the time a conversation begins, some assumptions may already be in the room.

For me, the pressure comes from wondering whether people are responding to what I’m saying or to what they assume about someone like me. Sometimes that means spending extra time thinking about how to phrase a question or get my point across.

Those feelings came to mind recently as I reread how Franklin, the Black character in *Peanuts*, was introduced. What stays with me is the reaction to his presence.

In 1968, educator Harriet Glickman encouraged Charles Schulz to include a Black character. Her correspondence, along with letters from Black parents, helped bring Franklin into the strip. His arrival drew appreciation and resistance. Schulz recalled a small number of intensely critical letters. [Schulz Museum](<https://schulzmuseum.org/remembering-harriet-glickman/>), [reader reactions](<https://schulzmuseum.org/celebrating-peanuts-online/>)

Even a fictional child’s place in an ordinary setting became something people challenged.

Experience and my relationships with others have helped ease that pressure. Working with people who allow time to think and welcome questions makes a difference.

I’m sharing this because I’m curious whether others recognize that feeling. The reasons people feel different vary, and so do the consequences. Understanding those differences can help us see more of what others may be navigating.

People respond to pressure differently, too. Some speak more when they feel a need to establish themselves. Others withdraw. Some are simply thinking. We cannot reliably tell which by watching.

Giving people time to respond and room to ask questions matters. A contribution doesn’t have to be immediate to be valuable, and asking for clarification shouldn’t cast doubt on someone’s ability.

Those choices shape whose ideas are heard and what people feel able to contribute.

**Have you ever held back because you worried about how you would be perceived? What helped you contribute more freely, and how can we offer that to others?**
